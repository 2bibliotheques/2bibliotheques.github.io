# Consider the Lilies of the Field: Lessons From September 2026

## 13 September 2026

At the time of writing, September is not yet halfway passed. The afternoons remain warm, and a green canopy still covers the land. Red and yellow fringes on the treetops are becoming more common, however, and some bushes and trees have unmistakably gained a uniform, yellow tinge. Seasonal change in these latitudes never ceases to bring with it an ephemeral and refreshing serenity that one always seems to forget in some way. Much less welcome than the fall weather has been the severe disquietude that marked my beginning of second-year university studies. Yet during the first week of my return to Cambridge, an angst that was all too similar to that which dominated the prelude to my first university academic year came again. A premature projection of difficulty and, most importantly, time-consumption onto my coursework again caused me to enter a state of mind that is simply not sustainable. Only this time, the folly of this fear and a return to a natural state of being has begun to come after only one week of readjustment and an academic year of prior experience.

The purpose of this entry is not to detail this disquietude of mine, rather it is to provide an insight that extends well beyond my personal situation to general periods of transition and uncertainty. The grounds of the Beaver Brook Reservation have once again been the setting of a remarkably sudden and striking revelation for me. This weblog entry will also provide a proper record of the beginning of my second year of university studies. As I sit waiting for the rains to cease on this day of rest, writing yet again proves to be a constructive use of leisure hours. It also happens to exercise a major academic and professional skill. Perhaps this entry will also permit me to better demonstrate a maxim that cannot be stressed enough to prospective university students and beyond; life continues upon transitioning to a new endeavour.

Some more context regarding my recent angst is required in order to fully understand the image that came upon me near Beaver Brook last Sunday. My coursework selection may be the most challenging yet, but the unwarranted fear that I had built up was instead due to the *time-consuming* aspect of three problem set courses. This fear has undoubtedly been punctuated by considering term-time employment and the job offers that I have received. Simply being unable to say with certainty that there would be enough time in each day to maintain a healthy sleep schedule, run, and have a *relative* day of rest each week caused me severe consternation. Working a job while studying was a goal of mine for this semester.  This was largely because of my concern that continuing without a term-time job would be taking advantage of my peculiar ability not to work whilst in school and that the cognitive labour from coursework merely equates to the consumption of knowledge.

This resolution was largely motivated by the abundance of weekend leisure hours during last semester. What I perceived to be a much less time-consuming schedule than the one I now have did make a light, part-time position more than feasible. Yet the schedule that I had imagined for this semester seemed to necessitate sacrifice of health or learning in order to work a part-time job. According to the [National Center for Labour Statistics](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/ssa/college-student-employment), only 40 percent of full-time students worked during the term in 2020. Yet that figure is much higher in my university—reportedly around 70 percent.

## 20 September 2026

With one week having passed since I wrote the text above, it has become clear that these consternations have merely created the conditions in which the three points that I will now share could be observed and appreciated for their profundity. Yet, as I now realise, lessons learned through difficulty are no less important to remember in times of relative ease.

Perhaps my first walk in the Massachusetts woods of this academic year was in the Beaver Brook Reservation weeks ago. Part of the reason why I travelled here first was to observe a passing Fitchburg Line train. As I walked along the dirt path in the small sliver of forest found in the lowest part of the reservation, I regarded the plants that must have reached their peak of growth before the withering of the fall. It was at that moment when I was struck by this thought; these plants exist out here at all times, and they will continue to live even as I return to a world away from this land in the endeavours of my modern life. This thought is especially profound for the glacial boulders strewn across the hills of Belmont. These stones have existed in an identical state since what I perceive to be the world long gone of the early 2010s, let alone the early 2000s. The timescales for which I might possess a true frame of reference stop there, letting alone the centuries and millennia past. These boulders and the land itself will continue to exist in such a state long after I am dust.

The beauty in this timelessness and serenity has inspired many of my writings here. In fact, this same trail was the one on which a similar revelation of my own ephemerality became the focus of a [previous entry](https://2bibliotheques.github.io/2026/03/31/granary.html) on this web site. In that case, however, I focused upon my impermanence in the land. Here, it is the permanence of the land itself that is worth remembering. That is because, only an instant after I had begun to muse upon this on the trail, a particular passage that I had thought of sometime before came to mind.

“Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they neither toil nor spin, yet I tell you, even Solomon in all his glory was not clothed like one of these.” ([Matthew 6:28-29]( https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%206&version=NRSVUE), NRSVUE)

One’s temporal concerns are certainly put into a certain perspective when considering the image of the immovable oak or, indeed, a patch of lilies blowing in a distant but familiar field. This is perhaps one of the reasons why walking amongst nature is so consistently calming to the mind. Even as recently as yesterday, a similar thought came upon me when considering the many lives that have passed along the shores of Spy Pond in Arlington. One may read on a worn historical plaque of the gaieties surrounding the pond from centuries ago, where a park along the Minuteman Bikeway still allows the public to gaze upon this treasure.

Whenever disquietudes arise that are inconsequential on a larger scale, thinking of life around Spy Pond or in a distant wilderness might provide a gateway towards clearer thought. I must stress that this is especially true when one’s worries are not well-founded. One need not consider the lilies of the field on a daily basis. Having concerns upon one’s mind is both natural and inevitable when doing work that matters.

That being said, another seemingly simple idea has proven to be deeply consequential and—perhaps most importantly—easy to hold an insidious disregard for. I find it only appropriate to continue on to the final sentence of the same chapter containing the excerpt above.

“So do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will bring worries of its own. Today’s trouble is enough for today.” ([Matthew 6:34]( https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%206&version=NRSVUE), NRSVUE)

As usual, the words of Christ here are resoundingly clear and simple. Yet immense complexity follows from the idea of actually putting these words into practice—or from the question of one’s capability to do so. These instructions should not be read as an interdiction of planning. So often does forethought and early action prevent worries from manifesting in the future. But there does exist a form of concern for the future that is both unhealthy and counterproductive. Perhaps that has been reflected somewhat in the beginning of this entry. There is a definite capacity to which one need only concern himself with day-to-day matters. It would certainly be imposing to see how much one will end up learning and doing in only a week of university studies on the Sunday before it begins. Having an appropriate concern for tomorrow, but no more, brings both peace to the mind and the probable ability to get even more accomplished.

The last of the three lessons from this month became especially clear to me today. I will take the liberty of introducing it myself; too much of anything good is bad. One must once again take care not to maintain an insidious veil of simplicity behind this idea that can be articulated with such brevity. There are myriad applications of this principle, but I am most immediately referring to coursework. The usual intrigue and fulfilment in problem solving or being exposed to new material will inevitably fade without some form of break. Be it a [20-minute walk]( https://2bibliotheques.github.io/2026/03/08/windowview.html) or a run, such pauses must sometimes be understood to be as important as the work that they interrupt. The rejuvenation that comes with observing a day of rest in some form continues to be remarkable. There are momentary yet probably inevitable times when one may question large-scale matters, such as when vague doubts came upon me regarding fulfilment in my prospective field of study this morning. Yet the excitement for continuing this work over the week to come that has come at the end of the same day—even in a state of slight illness—only further attests to the wholesomeness of setting work down. This is perhaps done in no way better than observing the day of the Lᴏʀᴅ.

## 26-27 September 2026

The autumnal equinox occurred this past Tuesday at 20:06 Eastern Standard Time. I write after a fruitful Saturday of coursework, sheltered from the rainstorm that will continue throughout this weekend. Increasingly cooler nights, the vibrant colours that now intersperse the treetops, and a general, unmistakable atmosphere outside signals the beginning of fall proper. Although this weblog was originally to conclude with some general remarks regarding university, this last full week of September has also marked the emergence of another revelation. It has constituted a lesson that is significant enough to be recorded here for other readers. I now detail this fourth principle.

University studies and modern life in general require one to keep track of information in a way that cannot be done without writing things down. One of the most effective ways to ensure that one’s goals and obligations are met is through a calendar. This is why the whiteboard has become an indispensable asset in my life, upon which I may plan the next day every evening. Planning in this manner brings forth a peace of mind and the ability to focus on endeavours within the day itself, albeit keeping certain longer-term goals that are pursued within each day in mind. As much as creating such a flexible outline of the next day has immense practical benefits, one can render this practice counterproductive by making the mistake that I might have been making continually throughout my university studies so far.

Even if one does not concern himself with tomorrow, an unhealthy worry regarding what will be accomplished throughout today can still manifest itself. A sure way to cause this worry is through setting a goal to complete more work than might be possible over the course of the day, followed by the failing to meet this goal. Although beginning and finishing coursework early is one of the best pieces of advice for prospective university students, it is in the pursuit of doing so that I have cultivated an unhealthy state of mind in recent weeks.

It was throughout this past week, however, when I recalled with greater clarity than ever a piece of advice that a church mentor once shared with our small group. The principle he shared is as follows; when planning out work to be done for the next day, one should plan to do only the minimum that is truly required. So often will one then accomplish more than what was planned. This advice might sound solid in itself, but soon afterwards I remembered another critical compliment to what he said. Upon planning the next day, he takes a moment to pray unto God that what he writes is good in His sight. So often have his hours been blessed to be more fruitful than he could have expected. *This* is trusting God with one’s time. I have struggled to do so since September of last year—and probably long before that.

An important implication follows from this planning and prayer. There is a certain peace and comfort that one may hold in the fact that as long as he does his best, he can do no more. Truly doing one’s best not only involves exerting all of his physical and mental efforts, but it generally involves focusing these efforts in the right areas at the right times. The grace and guidance of God prove especially necessary for this focus.

Deep and prolonged focus must indeed rank among the greatest practical gifts of all. I will conclude this entry by noting what may be one of the most underappreciated pieces of advice that is directly related to being able to focus, with implications extending much further to general quality of life and productivity. Receiving a proper amount of good-quality sleep must be a priority. University students might be at the greatest risk of falling under the impression that the recommended 7 to 9 hours of sleep for adults their age is rather a luxury. From the brain’s absorption of what one learns during the day to the ability to focus, I cannot properly convey the necessity of sleeping enough each night. I have provided two hyperlinks below from reputable university departments that should be viewed as a required reading for this piece. Below these two hyperlinks are two others that offer a brief sample of the importance of sleep for university students as seen through the scientific literature.

With that said, September 2026 has been the setting of select revelations that should persist during my university studies in years to come. Again, one’s focus must not stray too far beyond concerns on any given day. Too much of anything good will prove to be bad, and that includes planning out the next day. Finally, be it in the sliver of wilderness that might be one’s local greenway trail or in a forest proper, focusing momentarily on the timelessness of the land brings forth a peculiar peace. It is completely by God’s grace that I have thereby been able to consider the lilies of the field.
