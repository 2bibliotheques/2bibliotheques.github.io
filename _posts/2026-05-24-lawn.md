# On the Fruitlessness of Mowing Unused Lawn
## 24 May 2026

Spending the day working solely in the confines of one’s yard is perhaps one of the best ways that he may refamiliarize himself with his home. Many of my afternoon hours this first week home from university for summer recess were spent in such a manner. The majority of this time has doubtless been dedicated towards getting seeds into the ground for my garden. This relatively small area contains raised garden beds and another plot that has involved the most intensive labour—that being the initial breaking of the ground using a hoe-axe. It is probable that the blisters would be fewer, the soreness less severe if an adze hoe with a lighter head were used.
Nevertheless, this initial process of breaking ground is unequivocally the most laborious. The (albeit late) hoeing was facilitated this year by the previous tillage of the ground-level plot, although grasses had been allowed to overtake much of the garden over the course of the last summer after the failure of my spring wheat. I remarked how breaking new sod to expand the garden for this year was only marginally more difficult than doing so for the non-virgin soil. Yet, in the absence of an animal-driven plough that would normally be used only on larger fields of cereal crops, overcoming this first labour seems to have been unavoidable to tame the land for time immemorial. On a spring evening two years ago, shortly after I broke the sod for a new garden by hand for the first time, I observed with near elation what is better described as the same initial “digging” of a vegetable garden in a YouTube video. This was posted on a channel that is dedicated to Anglo-Saxon living history. I provide a hyperlink to this media here, where my reader may observe a rather cinematic rendition of the same rhythmic swinging of the hoe to break new ground from 4:00 onwards. The channel creator recruits a friend for the even more odious task of removing the roots of woody bushes earlier in the film. The video is best watched with subtitles.

From the no more than 30 square metres of this garden sprawled another task that I finished over the course of Monday. Or, rather, this was a task that I had elected to finish so that work could begin on the garden with the yard in good condition. I had reacquainted myself with every square metre of my home yard by line trimming and mowing it. Yet I had simultaneously been reacquainted with the copious amount of time that a complete mowing of my home yard demands; its devouring of the entire afternoon had caught me off guard. Near the end of my time mowing under the increasingly golden sun of the late afternoon, a thought struck me that is the ultimate reason for why I now write. It came upon me how much cumulative effort, petrol, money, and—most importantly—*time* I had expended over the course of previous summers mowing stretches of yard that would not be traversed thereafter until the next cutting of the grass. The semblance of a purpose with which open areas of grass that lay perpetually fallow are meticulously maintained collapsed inwards as soon as these musings begun.  

Yet the most time and petrol-consuming stretches of lawn mowing are often over these open spaces, whose productive quality goes no further than “looking nice.” This holds true for the small field composing more than half of my home’s acreage. To what extent is mowing this grass to practically the same length as that of the well-traversed areas closer to my home a time-consuming, fruitless, and almost Sisyphean task that one creates for himself? To what extent is this phenomenon magnified across the United States, burning untold quantities of petroleum and money that could be conserved or dedicated to more productive means? It is without question that the well-cut lawn is a modern cultural construct with a particularly strong character in the United States. Beyond the luxury and—what I must concede—the objective good of clear, tick-free lawn space for frequented areas, though, the greatest tragedy in the story of the lawn lies in mowing mostly unused land.

This is not to assert that a well-managed yard is any less of a necessity. What I am addressing in this piece may best be understood through an observation that I made only upon further reflection before writing this piece. I realised this morning that labouring in the garden and on the lawn during the same day may have led to this feeling of futility; I experienced the juxtaposition of one of the most quintessentially productive uses of land with an unproductive sink of time that is a mere excess of modern industrial society. The hours that one spends in the garden have a clear purpose in the eventual cultivation of useful plants. Even mowing smaller plots between structures such as one’s home, utility sheds, or patches of grass that is *actively used* for human recreation has a salient productive value. But it is in those spaces, when cultural norms or “aesthetics” alone compel one to expend his energy cutting grass, where countless hours may be saved.

Further still, these freed hours need not translate to less managed land. A small field like that of my home could be left to grow, for example, with the only regular mowing occurring along a series of paths. One might thus create a small, navigable trail system. Greater than the practical benefits of attracting pollinators or the environmental boons of less fossil fuel usage may be the bliss that one can take in the fact that such a field would demand *less of one’s time* mowing many unused square metres.

More alternatives to the weekly absurdity of mowing an unused patch of grass exist than I can provided a fitting overview for here. A common element among the majority of these alternatives, however, must be their bringing of one’s land closer to a natural state whilst saving precious time. I end this piece after being taken back once more during a favourite destination across the Ohio-Pennsylvania border early this afternoon. A certain, almost inarticulable serenity and timelessness accompanying spending time in the forest came upon me. The familiar yet ceaselessly temporal trails were quiet under the bright, overcast sky. Observations like those made in this piece are perhaps so valuable because of the prospects of dedicating the time that may be saved when informed by said observations to other endeavours—like that of hiking. I give all thanks and glory to God for this Sunday before Memorial Day and for these musings. Tomorrow marks a holiday that may be one of the most conspicuous in encouraging one to pause and remember.
