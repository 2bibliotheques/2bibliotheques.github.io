# On the Power of the Textbook
## 24 – 31 October 2026

As I have described before in this weblog, scaffolding and solidifying ideas into a written work on one’s free time is not only an enjoyable effort in itself, but this leisurely activity results in the creation of an arguably invaluable piece of text. Although October has once again marked the arrival of the first freezing mornings and the increasingly vibrant foliage, this past month has also been with learning for me—in the scope of coursework as well as in matters beyond. It is nearly hard to believe that I have declared my concentration in mathematics in the beginning of this same month and have now begun to seriously consider in what future career my labours might be most meaningfully applied.

This preoccupation may be the reason why the subject of this weblog entry has materialised only within this past week. Yet as will become clear, these musings are merely an affirmation of a truth that I have experienced firsthand throughout the past 2 years. This weblog entry is dedicated to the effectiveness of the textbook in the context of assigned and recommended readings for university courses—and in the world beyond. While this subject might seem peculiarly specific, the sometimes-unassuming nature of textbooks and the ease with which one may neglect spending time with them deserves the reflection that it receives here. I place emphasis on this latter observation, that being the joy and edification that one receives when simply spending time to *look closely* at a textbook—or any other thing for that matter—and the wholesomeness of doing so.  

I begin with two texts that have provided much of the immediate motivation for this piece. The introductory set theory, group theory, and real analysis course at my university provides the primary gateway to proof writing and abstract mathematical thought for the uninitiated. Weekly reading assignments from one of two textbooks cover the same material in the lecture, and problems in the respective “Chapter Exercises” often compose at least one problem in each weekly problem set. The course readings began in John Stillwell’s [*The Real Numbers*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-01577-4
) followed by Charles Pinter’s [*A Book of Abstract Algebra*](https://store.doverpublications.com/products/9780486474175
). These textbooks appear to provide an excellent introduction to the course content, and I can at least affirm that they are clear and well-written. Perhaps in no other discipline more than mathematics does one need the time to mull upon new concepts and to re-expose himself to them. This fact, combined with the course structure that I have described, has made the power of the textbook apparent to me in doing more than supplementing in-class lectures.

So often has my understanding of the concepts central to an in-class lecture been merely deepened after reading the textbook beforehand. Yet, equally as often, the professor’s alternative presentation of a concept in lecture resolves its opaqueness upon reading the textbook. So too are foundational concepts learned through the textbook frequently extended to new material that would otherwise cause confusion. This applies not only to this particular course, but the same has held in my linear algebra and introductory mechanics classes for this semester.

In all three of these classes, however, the importance of doing more than idle reading has reared itself repeatedly. One must not consider a topic in the text as mastered until he has gone through the textbook exercises. Problem-solving exposes shallow understandings and allows one to truly absorb the material. I encourage my reader to follow Dr. David Morin’s advice given in the preface pages of [*Problems and Solutions in Introductory Mechanics*]( https://davidmorin.physics.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum12331/files/2025-10/mechanics_preface.pdf) and [*Special Relativity for the Enthusiastic Beginner*]( https://davidmorin.physics.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum12331/files/2025-10/relativity_preface.pdf). Morin’s advice applies to textbooks well beyond physics, and his warning in *Special Relativity…*  to never dismiss a problem as solvable without fully working through it is especially resonating. Indeed, the ability to read the solutions to textbook problems only adds to the versatility of a text *when this ability is used correctly.* As one of my mathematics professors instructs his students, one usually does not have a true understanding of a concept until he can teach it to someone else through writing a lecture-like explanation.

This writing on the effectiveness of the textbook is not to serve as mere reflection, however. I advise the university student to *take the time* for focused reading of assigned textbook sections. From my university experience thus far, reading the textbook is akin to making an investment of time. In regard to doing assigned readings and in general, studying over the course of the semester allows one to learn material for the long-term and makes examination periods a time to fill gaps in knowledge rather than to cram.

The power of the textbook in a university context further illuminates how effective a good book can be for those self-studying a subject at any point in life. One must block out the time to spend with a textbook and any other text to truly appreciate its contents. Some may assert this to be exceedingly difficult; that is completely understandable. Yet it is worth considering that part of the reason why one cannot find such time might not lie in the idiosyncrasies of his schedule. Rather, this dearth of time may reflect the life of a modern citizen in general. This leads me to some further musings that have come upon me more than once over this month.

Closely studying a concept or a material simply takes *time*. The month of thought and reflection required before finalising the subject of this weblog entry might reflect that. I have needed to acknowledge perhaps more than ever the need to put off non-urgent endeavours like this writing that require a sufficiently large amount of time. Several articles and websites that I discovered throughout this month thereby remain to be read. This is not to say that the semester has been “too busy” or that I could not have made the time to do these things weeks ago. It is instead a matter of prioritisation that my “To Read” list has only grown throughout the month. (As an aside, I highly recommend that my reader keep such a list—even for websites or journal articles that seem of passing interest. Through close looking one may very well find an excerpt or a piece of information that he cherishes forevermore.) As rewarding as my university studies continue to be, a full-time courseload has made it clear to me that one of the greatest pleasures in life is to have the time to study, write, and read.

One of the articles currently at the top of my list is relatively short, yet it is dense with profundity. I recalled the striking result of deriving Euler’s formula from my first year of university studies when it was mentioned in passing during a physics section (we were reviewing spring oscillation differential equations). After a brief internet search, one of the highest-quality articles on Euler’s formula seems to be the one [hyperlinked here](https://mathvault.ca/euler-formula/). Once may imagine the time that those like Euler must have spent closely observing that which could so easily be overlooked or taken for granted. Indeed, it is easy to take for granted many of the astounding results that now form the foundations of one’s science and mathematics education starting in grade school. Regardless, I am not asserting that the modern industrial citizen does not possess enough free time. In fact, quite the opposite may be true. Yet it is still worth considering how those in the past found the time—or the space—to study things so carefully. [Tycho Brahe]( https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/brahe.html) and his unfathomably precise observations of the sky before the advent of the telescope provides a good example.

I leave my reader with an image that is directly related to something that I intend to write upon further when the time permits. On several weekday mornings in recent weeks, as the deep blue of the morning twilight begins to brighten, I have heard the sound of a bell coming from Porter Square to the northwest. It has at times been quiet enough to additionally hear the familiar rumble that has been featured on this weblog before. I am hearing the sound of an inbound commuter rail train on these quiet mornings. Not only do railways create fascinating stretches of landscape, but the ability to voyage on the commuter rail from Boston to places as distant as Portland, Maine imparts a certain intrigue when I have the opportunity to watch a train pass. Active MBTA commuter rail trains also report real-time data giving their locations, lead cars, and destinations. A comprehensive web site that I sometimes use to anticipate incoming trains may be found [here](https://mbta.sites.fas.harvard.edu/T/commuter-rail-map.html), followed by the [official MBTA commuter rail page]( https://www.mbta.com/schedules/commuter-rail). The $10 commuter rail weekend pass is a remarkable service that I intend to use when the time presents itself.

I finished writing this weblog entry in the Harvard Graduate School of Education Library. Outside of the floor-to-ceiling windows, I saw the sun approaching tree-level for the last time in October of 2026. Given that today is October 31st, I leave my reader with a final website to peruse that is hyperlinked [here]( https://www.luther.de/en/index.html). Indeed, as I took a brief walk along the Fitchburg Cutoff Bike Path under the setting sun, it struck me that the serene beauty of the vividly pigmented, yet balding late fall trees would have looked much the same in the otherwise different world that was 1517.
