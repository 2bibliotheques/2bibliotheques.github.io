### I. Introduction

There exists a certain, wholesome clarity that comes upon the mind when walking that is not limited to walking in the wilderness. One would be hard-pressed to experience this bliss strolling along a stroad lined with big-box stores and fast-food chains, of course, but such built environments are inherently not intended for humans. They are intended for cars. Perhaps that is why walking in the local neighbourhood, on a street lined with the domiciles of others and free from heavy car traffic, first comes to my mind when thinking of an enjoyable walk in a developed area. My first summer recess has indeed been interspersed with memorable walks in the relatively quiet neighbourhoods near home.

Yet walking in the neighbourhood also provides one with a peculiar opportunity to closely observe the structures and landscapes along well-travelled routes. True to the nature of automobile travel, the perception that one has out of the windows of a speeding car will obscure any detailed observations and the heightened appreciation of the landscape that results. This phenomenon holds true even if the elements of the landscape are perceived on a daily basis, namely the houses.

My family and I have consequentially taken joy in looking at the homes of others during our neighbourhood walks. Moreover, I often observe others’ houses through the lens of an aspiring homeowner. The predominant question that I often ponder is if I could envision myself living in them over the long-term. And a thorough answer to this question requires a clear conception of the ideal house. I have attempted to address what constitutes this model in my previous writings, but only after recent walks has the need to formalise the ideal house model become pressing.

Along with food and water, shelter ranks among the fundamental human needs. With these needs so easily met in modern society, however, it may now be more important than ever to place careful thought into how they are sated. Much like how the consumer in the modern industrial food chain may not view his food purchases through the lens of investing in what will ultimately compose his cells, part of the impetus for this essay is the extension of this perspective to the twenty-first century domicile.

In this piece, I set forth the salient qualities of the ideal house as well as the specific structural elements and appliances that it should have. This vision of the ideal house will serve as a future reference for me, but the perspective through which I arrive at several key concepts further reveals more general qualities of the ideal house that all must consider. A formalisation of this ideal model will be useful to one choosing among a set of any houses on the market, where these general qualities may be most useful. The location of a house is arguably one of the most important considerations that will additionally be addressed. The final part of this piece accordingly touches upon the detrimental effects of the commute and why travel times between work and home must be minimised. As will be shown, the ideal house presented in this piece is well within the bounds of reason and practicality.

## II. Characteristics of the Ideal House

The model of the ideal house presented here draws upon a basis of universal guiding characteristics. Since this writing concerns the ideal house for *permanent* habitation, it follows that sustainability is a chief concern. It is so much so that every other general quality and specific functional element of the ideal house that will be addressed are ultimately intended to make living in the house sustainable.

It follows that the ideal house should be environmentally, financially, and practically sustainable. A rigorous delineation between these three facets of sustainability is beyond the scope of this piece. Furthermore, these categories are intertwined in each element of the modern house. No residential building in the latitudes from which I write would be complete without heating, for example, and the choice of how one heats his house will be a focus in the next section.

The reasoning used in deliberating central heating is useful in clarifying the latter form of sustainability that I mentioned, however—that being practicality. The ideal house should remain habitable without the need for a constant input of electricity, gas, or water. Although it will be assumed that utility services will ordinarily be accessible, no house should be rendered uninhabitable if these services are disrupted. This aspect of practical sustainability will be pursued when discussing central heating. Practical sustainability also becomes of primary concern when considering aging. One must ask himself if he will be able to navigate and maintain his house when his motor faculties are degraded many decades in the future.

The ideal house should thus be maintainable. Beyond the money saved in repairs that may be categorised under financial sustainability, prioritising an easily maintainable house brings forth another guiding principle that is used extensively here. The maintainability of the ideal house *saves time*. Minimising the amount of time that one must spend maintaining his house is desirable as a means to dedicate more time to other life endeavours. One may argue that this line of reasoning precludes purchasing a house large enough to fit his conception of what is ideal. Yet the ideal house that is formulated here rests upon one more fundamental principle that follows from sustainability and maintainability.

The ideal house should embody a mindset of contentedness rather than superfluity. A house that maximises sustainability while meeting the needs of its inhabitants in the modern era may be most conspicuously limited in its dimensions. This aspect of the ideal house is more closely examined below. It may be tempting to dismiss the notion of being merely content with one’s abode when countless houses on the market seem to offer so much more. Anything less than a two-story house with room to spare may seem unfitting for individuals above a certain income threshold. The question of if a feature of a house is needed to live contentedly for the rest of one’s life concerns more than saving a portion of household income, however. As will become evident when I enumerate the specific elements that the ideal house should have, it is a luxury to own a shelter that fully meets one’s needs *and no more*. These needs include certain wants that are arguably necessary for modern life, such as a space for quiet study or for storing media.

Finally, the fine model of the ideal house will unequivocally vary between its users. Yet, as my conceptualisation of the ideal house will show, the guiding principles set forth above should not vary. Each may modify or derive his own requirements when choosing the ideal house just as I have after many occasions for reflection. Those with families will axiomatically find the model in need of serious adjustment.

Before continuing, I must stress the fact that the ideal house model outlined here is—as all models are—an ideal. Too many houses exist on the market that stray far from these principles, and it would be both unreasonable and irresponsible to suggest that every reader build his own shelter that meets the ideal. As will be touched upon later, the ideal house more assuredly remains an indispensable tool in the home-buying process. Making modifications to many extant houses will allow one to meet the standards of the model at that. Exactly what features the ideal house should have will now be elucidated according to the above standards.

## III. Specific Implementations of the Ideal House

Beginning with the general layout of the ideal house, a specific image repeatedly comes to mind. One must picture the simple log cabin that a settler of a place like the Connecticut Western Reserve or western New England would have lived in. The simple, thatched-roof peasant homes of medieval to early modern Europe are another possible model. Yet much of the inspiration that should be drawn from these structures is not found in their literal composition or construction methods. Rather, it is the utilitarian nature of these domiciles and the contented state in which their inhabitants were likely able to live that places modern homebuying into perspective. The actual dimensions of these shelters present more than abstract inspiration, however. Such houses of yore offer a model in their single-storey construction with a relatively small footprint. The front porch that was found on some settler cabins in my homeland of the Northwest Territory additionally remains relevant as a dry outdoor space.

The fact that these small houses were often built directly by their inhabitants once again raises the point of the maintainability of the ideal house and the resulting saved time. Those who lived in these shelters spent much of their time, as most people did for most of human history, growing food. The cumulative hours of mowing a large lawn (which is a subject of its own that [I have examined before](https://2bibliotheques.github.io/2026/05/24/lawn.html)) or cleaning many square metres of unused interior space would simply have been impractical. Of course, one must take care not to overextend this argument. I am not suggesting that a modern two-story house of reasonable size demands so much more time to maintain that the benefits of added interior space are outbalanced. Along with the fact that is generally easier to maintain a single storey house—roof repairs are a good example—the time that is saved by the single storey ideal is nevertheless accompanied by several other serious considerations in terms of sustainability. Stairs are certain to present a serious obstacle to accessing half of one’s house when she grows old. Accessibility is thus a component of sustainability. As I will soon address, another principal consideration to make for the size of a house concerns central heating.

In addition to its dimensions, the ideal house should be composed of materials that are maximally durable and thermally insulative. Slate roofing is the ideal in this regard. Although tile roofing provides an alternative option, freeze-thaw cycles necessitate synthetic tiling if tile is chosen. Houses in places like England provide a strong inspiration; moss-strewn roofs that have not been replaced in over 100 years provide a practical testament to the viability of a stone or ceramic-based roof. In a similar light, exterior walls composed of load-bearing brick or stone are ideal for the same reasons. Choosing more natural materials of this nature has added environmental benefits. Environmental sustainability resulting from well-insulating materials augments the general sustainability of heating features to be discussed.

Even on the hottest days, the ideal house should likewise be free from air conditioning. British houses again provide a good model, as most have done well without this energy-intensive apparatus. A sensible deployment of fans and exterior window awnings is remarkably effective in keeping a house cool. It is unfortunate that these solutions are now underappreciated in the United States, where further “cultural norms” for housing seem to impede the widespread implementation of other apparatuses discussed in this piece. The only logic according to which many of these solutions are not the most reasonable choice seems to be that favouring luxury paired with wants over pressing environmental concerns paired with needs.

It is a matter of practicality rather than luxury to have an ample distribution of windows throughout every room, however. A house should be filled with natural light during the daytime hours, even on overcast days. Beyond the obvious need for visibility, an indoor atmosphere that is in sync with the solar cycle facilitates a healthy circadian rhythm. This effect is especially pronounced after sundown when camping in the wilderness, where one may feel especially tired despite the potentially early hour. A sunlit house is wholesome for the mind regardless. It follows from above that the windows of the ideal house should be open for much of the summer months, providing yet another connection with the external environment. Most simple, well-insulated windows of modest proportions currently on the market should be sufficient. Thorough natural lighting may be the only quality discussed here that most modern houses already possess. 

The final major exterior structural element to the ideal house might not be considered part of the main structure at all. I am referring to an enclosed garage that is large enough for one’s vehicles as well as a small workshop. Manually operated swinging doors provide a simple solution for the vehicle entryway; many rural European homes provide a suitable real-world reference yet again. The ability to place one’s vehicles in the garage must be stressed here. Another widespread practice near my home that seems to defy pragmatic thinking is the parking of cars directly outside of a garage. For a culture so centred on the automobile, the question of how much weathering of a vehicle comes not from driving, but from sitting exposed to the elements seems to be rarely asked. And it appears that most online sources assert that this weathering is significant. Although I have not been able to locate a formal study with a brief search, I provide one of the many articles that I found [hyperlinked here](https://www.firestonecompleteautocare.com/blog/driving/garage-vs-outdoor-parking/).

The interior layout of the ideal house will be largely limited by its external form. Sustainability again dictates the principal considerations to be made. Because the individualised rendition of the ideal house considered in this piece only accommodates one to two persons, the only rooms that the ideal house should most probably have are listed below:
 
-	An attic
-	A storage cellar
-	A water closet
-	A bathroom
-	A bedroom
-	A living room
-	A kitchen
-	A study

The last four rooms are of special concern. Foremost, the ideal house should not contain dividing walls between these four areas. A centralised space in which one spends most of his hours should receive priority for central heating, reinvoking the often-single-room shelters of the past. I must again stress that this is most suitable for a single inhabitant. The reason why heating holds so much weight here will be clarified shortly.

But I must elaborate why the final room on the list is arguably a requisite space in any modern house. A study with a desk and media storage area allows one to read, manage finances, write, hold phone calls, and complete the myriad other essential home office tasks of the twenty-first century in a dedicated space. The study does not appear to be a staple of American home interior design. That may be because the efficiency resulting from placing office and library apparatuses in a centralised area, along with the privacy that the home office provides, may not currently be widely considered. The Wikipedia article accessible through [this hyperlink]( https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Study_(room)) provides a more thorough overview of the space.

A guide in the Paul Revere House once remarked to my family and I that high ceilings in a house were once a status symbol; they indicated that the owner could afford to heat the extra space. Indeed, the ceilings in the Paul Revere House are just high enough to comfortably accommodate a 1.8 metre individual. This observation holds some value for conceptualising a sustainable ideal house. It is directly related to central heating, that being one of the most important elements that I have yet to address.

Central heating is a topic that deserves its own piece (see [“On the Hearth and the Granary”](https://2bibliotheques.github.io/2026/03/31/granary.html)). In light of the principles outlined above, however, the ideal house should certainly possess a log-burning wood stove capable of heating most of the interior living space. Although it is hoped that one would occasionally heat his home using such a stove for the experience itself (and for marginal savings in utility expenses), a wood stove would most practically be reserved as an emergency heating option for most modern houses. This is why the ideal house should still be equipped with a wood stove even if forests of accessible firewood are not in the immediate vicinity. Once again, the mere fact that a loss of utility services may render a house practically uninhabitable without serious modification is the primary motivator for this requirement. In the event of even a temporary loss of service, a wood stove would be worth the investment. Although one may still hope that the wood stove rests dormant, life in the house becomes assuredly more sustainable with one installed. Considering the small footprint of the ideal house, installing a sufficient wood stove configuration would also be well within reason.

Heating in the ideal house should normally be automated, as it is in most modern buildings in the latitudes from which I write. The boiler provides the most practical and efficient central heating method at this level. Hot water radiators heat rooms more evenly with lower energy consumption compared to the forced air of a furnace. One need only refer to most modern houses in Britain and northern Europe once more; boilers are the standard there. It is both more financially and more environmentally sustainable to operate a boiler for half of the year rather than a furnace.

The ideal house should also possess a tankless water heater, that being a similar technology that has yet to be duly appreciated in the United States. It is beyond the scope of this piece to discuss the boons of only using hot water when it is absolutely necessary. Yet, along with the thorough comparison provided by [this Consumer Reports article](https://www.consumerreports.org/appliances/water-heaters/tankless-water-heaters-vs-storage-tank-water-heaters-a5291982593/) between tankless and conventional water heaters for residential use, it becomes self-evident that maintaining a tank full of hot water in one’s house should no longer be necessary.

A more general principle guides the choice of appliances in the ideal house. Every apparatus, such as the dishwasher or the oven, need only be a one or two-time investment. Lifetime appliance durability may seem unrealistic to the modern homeowner, and this is perfectly reasonable when referring to the consumer appliance market. That is why the ideal home should contain commercial-grade equipment wherever feasible. This is a relatively minor consideration given the principles outlined above as well as the expense and potential difficulty of acquiring commercial appliances. Yet it is intuitive that equipment designed for high-frequency public use should be remarkably long-lasting and easy to maintain for a single household.

I extend this reasoning to the design of certain rooms, namely the kitchen and the bathroom. Sealed subway tiles spanning the floor to the ceiling of the bathroom are a particular design element worth considering. Not only is this ceramic surface easy to clean, but the hygienic benefits compared to the porous, moisture-trapping surfaces of wood or painted drywall are significant. Public restrooms are often designed in this way for good reason. Cleanliness and maintainability for traffic of 50 persons per day translates to even greater cleanliness and maintainability in a single house.

Most modern houses also fail to take advantage of their rainwater management systems. I am principally referring to the rainwater that is channelled from the surface area of their roofs. Rainwater collection barrels are likely to provide all that one needs to water his garden or other plants that need intensive care. Although this is another minor implementation, such reservoirs at the ends of a house’s gutters are easily installable, remarkably pragmatic, yet surely underappreciated. This remains true even at the homes of many gardeners.

Finally, the ideal house should possess a sustainable and practical waste separation system. The ability to discard almost all waste in a single bin that is then emptied away, travelling beyond sight and mind immediately afterward, is a recent and comparatively absurd phenomenon. It is only logical to separate recyclable and compostable waste into different receptacles. While the environmental and practical benefits of doing so are beyond the scope of this writing, waste composting may require a special apparatus. The ideal house should possess an outdoor compost bin, especially in the absence of a commercial composting service. Significant volumes of dustbin space may be saved by separating food waste and recycling alike. Yet composting further provides rich fertilizer that may either be used in the garden or shared with those needing it.

## IV. A Note on the Commute

An ideal house model that is hardly applicable to the real world would hardly be worth the time to conceptualise here. Once again, at least some of the features of the ideal house enumerated above are more than practical to implement through modifications to many pre-existing residences. The ideal house described here is, in fact, primarily envisioned to be a product of extensive renovation rather than new construction; environmental, financial, and practical sustainability take precedent. Yet the very fact that the ideal house may be derived from countless structures already on the market leads to what may be the most important consideration on this list—where the ideal house is located.

Location is what truly completes the ideal house so that it becomes the ideal home. This is why location may also be where my ideal house model deviates the most from that of others with respect to personal preference. I cannot hide the fact that avoiding the noise and pollution of an urban environment in favour of the tranquillity of a smaller city or town ranks high among my priorities for a permanent residence. Chief among the factors that will dictate where I and most others live, however, is the presence of job opportunities. The commute thus becomes of chief concern.

The question of the commute transcends idiosyncrasies in choosing a location for the ideal house. The commute is yet another subject that deserves a dedicated piece. To be succinct, a commute of no more than 15 minutes in one direction should be prioritised. Any home outside of the radius of a 30-minute commute should be excluded from consideration. 
It is understandable why the weight that I assert the commute should have in the home selection process seems unreasonable when one merely considers his schedule in the scope of a day or a week. Yet over a longer term, hours of time lost in transit accumulate. One need only imagine the potential of an extra 1 to 2 hours of focused time every week to acquire a new skill, for example. That some individuals commute nearly 1 hour in a single direction every day never ceases to be astounding. 

Some may argue that these individuals are left with no other option; and in certain cases, that is true. But it is likewise true that if the commute was as large of a consideration in choosing where to live as is propounded here, just as many of these instances of would likely be avoided. No endeavour compatible with the concentration needed to drive a car—such as listening to an audiobook—can sufficiently justify losing so much time. Consider the case where an individual listens to an audiobook in her house and does nothing more for 45 minutes in the place of a long commute. The individual herself would likely be vying to either read a physical book or to pair this activity with something more.

Whatever activities one may do in her vehicle to justify subjecting herself to the length of the commute merely soothe the pain caused by the unavoidable fact that so much time is cumulatively lost. For many Americans, commuting further means navigating the U.S. highway system, precariously enclosed precariously in a metal box with human interaction limited to nothing more than the faceless exteriors of other cars. Indeed, the exorbitant commute lengths endured by some Americans are an excess of modern technology, and the cultural deformities wrought by the automobile that make these commute lengths seem acceptable are principally to blame here.

## V. Conclusion

I set out to write this piece in order to more rigorously specify what the ideal house is. And, I hope, that has now been finished. What this piece has almost certainly failed to do, however, is provide an irrefutable defence of the properties that I have used to define the ideal house. That is why I must conclude by reiterating two qualifications to the scope of this essay. The ideal house is, once again, an ideal by definition. What may separate this model from others, however, is the serious feasibility of a middle-class member of modern society being able to achieve all that the ideal house demands. Nonetheless, there almost certainly exist houses in a similar climate to my homeland that already embody practically every quality of the ideal house. 

I must once again stress that every individual’s conception of the ideal house will probably differ. But one of the principal characteristics of a good model is its adaptability to other circumstances. From low ceilings to the use of a boiler for central heating, the elements of the ideal house discussed throughout this piece have primarily been reached following logical and arguably universal principles. The ideal house suited to almost any other inhabitant will thus be much more similar than it is different to the ideal house conceptualised here.

This piece will be the last that I write during the summer university recess period for 2026. Although it is long, this text was a pleasure to write and revise. My conception of the ideal house will most probably be altered before I ever consider choosing a permanent home. It is also most probable that these alterations will merely be additive. In the face of the greed and consumerism pervading the cultures of many modern nations, environmental, practical, and financial sustainability remain principles to live by. I thank my reader for reaching this point and for having read this long entry with an open mind. Like a satisfying mathematical proof, I may now refer to this formalised model of the ideal house when gazing upon homes for the joy of doing so during walks. And there remain many more neighbourhood walks to take, God willing.
