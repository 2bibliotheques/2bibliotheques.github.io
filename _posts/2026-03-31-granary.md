# On the Hearth and the Granary
## 29 – 31 March 2026

### 29 March 2026

Yesterday afternoon was tranquil in the southern half of the Beaver Brook Reservation. The sunshine accentuated the bare oaks standing among the pale green grass whose new shoots were just beginning to overcome the brown strands of last year’s growth. I walked along a small patch of forest in that half of the park, noting a familiar smell of Earth that must have been the leaf litter warming in the light. This was the same trail that I had traversed in February when I reflected upon my impermanence in the landscape, and I later made a certain observation in my first-year journal that deserves further consideration. I made a note this past week to write about this idea along with another that remain especially relevant as the spring weather intensified. Yet the significance of the trail that I visited that afternoon only came upon me after I returned home.

I am referring to the centrality that the fire and the granary have had in sustaining human life on this land (and the in the general climate of my home) up until recent times. This entry will further these reflections that have been recorded in my personal writings, although I must also note that it comes at the end of an especially blessed and thought-provoking weekend—culminating in Palm Sunday.

I wrote on February 1st that all of those enjoying the land around me that afternoon—including myself—were completely dependent upon food and heating sourced from places far from the Earth being tread on. It was a “disturbing yet enlightening” realization that, if the long and complex chain of industrial processes upon which we depended was disrupted, the woods around Beaver Brook Reservation would probably be forever rid of our presence within a few weeks or months. Perhaps this was another way of characterising my visit in the woods as an excursion from my life elsewhere, especially when the impending sunset was likely limiting my time on the trail.

A peculiar sense of comfort may be found by referring to the way in which people have lived on this land in times now long past, however. There was nothing in those woods—neither on that day or on this relatively warm, March evening—that one could eat. I briefly remarked an increasing dependence upon stored grain for one’s sustenance that would have normally manifested at that time of year. Such a dependence would be even more pronounced right now, as winter stores would need to stretch well beyond this time when the tree buds are beginning to appear in greater number.

The storage of dry grains, such as emmer wheat or spelt, is a remarkable way to ensure a supply of food that I did not internalize until relatively recently (modern refrigeration has precluded the general need for me to do so). Two useful resources that have spurred my interest may be found in this reproduced article by [Edward Green from The Building Conservation Directory, 2004]( https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/_gsdata_/_saved_/granaries/granaries.htm) and the [Wikipedia page]( https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granary) for the granary. A close reading of these will give more context than I can here. I will only end with the remark that life once flowed from these structures. The granary and the association it had with survival into the spring have become yet another long-gone part of life here.

### 31 March 2026

A similar phenomenon regarding the hearth in one’s dwelling and the general role of wood fires in daily life deserves further reflection. I will attempt to capture my meditations thereon rather briefly.

I have likely been outdoors this past winter more than ever before. Yet at the end of every excursion, my dependence upon a heated shelter became increasingly evident to me. As is so often the case in modern life, it is worth the effort required to ground oneself in where his fundamental means of sustenance are most basically sourced. Beyond [finding the answer that I was looking for]( https://www.energyandfacilities.harvard.edu/utilities/steam) after some brief research with this question in mind, though, I once again sought a degree of comfort in the way that people had lived in the past without modern dependencies.

From the wigwams that once dotted this landscape to a typical Anglo-Saxon home (I am thinking of a high-quality video series documenting an Anglo-Saxon pit house that is [hyperlinked here]( https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3OjSGJ-LLLE)), the hearth had literally been at the centre of their inhabitants’ domestic lives. Cooking and drying clothing are perhaps just as important uses of the hearth as the warmth it can provide to a well-insulated or small enough abode. *Here* lies the answer to living and thriving without modern heating. It is remarkable how much heat can tangibly be found in a log or merely in sticks strewn on the forest floor (sticks that happen to be kept especially dry in the deep winter months).

I will leave my reader with one final principle. Among the many skillsets that are no longer required in modern life, fire starting and fire building may be the most beneficial to “relearn.” More specifically, the value of knowing how to start fires with nothing more than the means that would have been available to those thousands of years ago should not be understated. Not only do I find that learning how to do so would be fun, but the ability to source a fire using only materials that can be found in the local, natural environment—or materials simple enough to have been traded for, such as flint, steel, and charcloth—would be a skill to cherish. People would have needed to start fires on a regular basis, something that, along with my own experience starting campfires, attests to the importance of consistent practice that so often holds true for a rewarding skill.
