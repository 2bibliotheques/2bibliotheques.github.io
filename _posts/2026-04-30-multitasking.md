# On Canaday Hall, Multitasking, and Spring
## 26-30 April 2026
## Revised 1 May 2026

### 26-27 April 
It has been nearly one month since I have written here. Even before committing my latest change to this web site’s GitHub repository, however, a matter that demanded further meditation through writing was already on my mind. It was to my immense pleasure on the morning of “Housing Day” at my university when I learned that I will be a resident of the Currier House student dormitory complex—as I prefer to describe it—for the next three academic years.

To have guaranteed heated room and board regardless of what dormitory I received endowed a certain light-heartedness to my preference for a particular dormitory complex in the Radcliffe Quadrangle. Seemingly because of its obscure name and its marginally greater proximity to the [Cooper-Frost-Austin House](https://www.historicnewengland.org/property/cooper-frost-austin-house/), Pforzheimer House became my future “house preference.” Yet to live with such accommodations as heated showers genuinely made any serious concern about this matter frivolous. Given the supposedly random dormitory assignment complex, I did not even bother researching the “houses” and have yet to visit all of them. I will note, however, that a measure of quietness and tranquillity being at a further distance from Harvard Square and Cambridge Central made a complex situated in the Radcliffe Quadrangle especially appealing. Now, I pray, I will be able to enjoy that. My concerns about the time that I will lose commuting between the Radcliffe Quadrangle and Harvard Yard have been ameliorated throughout this past month through the power of repeated measurement; the entire process of readying my bicycle and travelling between Canaday Hall and Currier House via bicycle lanes and sidewalks consistently takes no more than 10 minutes.

A dormitory open house event for rising sophomores occurred on the same night of housing decisions being released, and that is where a certain disquietude accompanying a separation from the ease of mind found at home after sunset came upon me. I then realised that I was longing to return to my hallway single room in Canaday. The thought that home could be found in what I have learned is one of the busiest and loudest parts of the Harvard campus remains peculiar to me. Yet this noise and activity will not be a part of live in Harvard Yard that I miss. I have perhaps only been able to counteract, namely through thorough light and sound blocking measures to maintain a quiet sleeping environment. I continue to be woken, at times, to the exasperation of hearing voices or laughter at some time after midnight.

This episode has nevertheless strengthened my appreciation for my current living arrangements. I am beyond content with the shelter that I have been gifted with. “Housing Day” has likewise given me occasion to research the history of my home, namely using [this resource](https://harvardplanning.emuseum.com/sites/924/canaday-hall) (I have yet to explore all of the cited materials in depth). Indeed, the only “house pride” that I now claim is that for the perfect adequacy of Canaday Hall. A home remains something that is ideally permanent, however, something that the awkward range of sentiments evoked by the prospects of leaving these temporal accommodations has evidenced.

### 27 – 29 April
The dearth of weblog entries this April is not because this past month has been uneventful. Part of the reason why I have not written may actually rest in one of the most significant lessons that I have learned in my first year of undergraduate studies. The realisation that struck me partially last Friday and fully this past Monday has provided a remarkable sense of clarity and exigence for the creation of this blog post. I have realised how many of the concerns I wrote of in my physical journal throughout this past month are a direct consequence of this matter.

I have spent countless hours since spring break reading and assaying to construct a mathematical model for my team of three’s final project in an introductory applied mathematics course. My choice of coupling the 2-Process Model of Sleep Regulation to ambient room temperature changes continues to make this project especially difficult and time-consuming. But this final project need by no means be remarkably complicated. I have allowed this project to occupy my mind excessively and disproportionately. In so doing, it is likely that I have only made this endeavour more difficult and time consuming. The progress that I have made in recent days attests to this fact—and to the bad habit which I will finally address directly. I have fallen into the trap of multitasking.

The folly of multitasking has been the subject of my writing before. Yet, upon reflection, a perceived urgency to make progress with the Applied Math 50 project has only provided the strongest means to plague myself with multitasking, particularly in the last two weeks. I could not help but feel that my physical journal entries this past Sunday—those about the need to focus more on the environment around me whilst running or about the remarkable clarity of mind I was given on a walk this last Thursday—did not adequately answer for all that could be said. Only during my run on Monday afternoon did it strike me what I had been missing. As I simply focused on the blooming leaves or any other matters beyond coursework, I realised that I had been multitasking whilst running for the past month or more.

That said, there is no shame in allowing one’s labours to occupy his mind in the middle of the working day. Musing upon a particular problem when the chance arises can be helpful, and it is frankly unavoidable in the course of learning. Yet the mental multitasking that I have allowed is not of this productive kind. Beyond physical fitness and the associated benefits to one’s mind, running is an indispensable means for focused recreation and leisure in the midst of the day. It is a chance to simply experience the natural environment in a manner reminiscent of walking in the woods. Yet I have found myself running without *perceiving* what is around me especially often in the past month or more. Thinking of an applied mathematics project or of other coursework too extensively detracts from the break and full refreshment that an activity like running provides. This is especially true when the only significant progress on a task will occur through focused time at a desk with relevant materials at hand.

I have observed fellow students distracting themselves with other coursework or media within my Applied Math 50 course throughout this semester, most notably during paper presentation days. Yet even when one may easily choose to do so when the opportunity to passively listen to “unproductive” material relative to another assignment is given, the benefits of dedicating one’s full attention to matters like these are manifold. It is a folly to work under conditions of distraction or half-focus, especially as one deprives himself of the clarity of mind in returning to a problem after a break.

These writings may give one the impression that my schedule is especially busy. But this is not so. I do not live a life where multitasking is needed. My lacking clarity of mind and focus upon matters beyond coursework in these past weeks should not have occurred. Yet after having been gifted with these realisations that I alone could not seem to have pinpointed, the peace of mind and a reinvigoration in my labours has been remarkable. From the writing of this blog post to the return of a sense of timelessness while simply observing the slices of a natural environment that I get to see throughout the week, the benefits of denying multitasking in all of its forms have in other words been immediate. I cannot give enough thanks to God for this resolution. Although it has manifested on the cusp of the end of my first year at Harvard—the last official day of classes has become the last night during the course of this writing—the folly of multitasking may be one of the most significant revelations during this past year for me.

I have gone from an apparent lack of weblog topics to more than this evening will permit me to write about. The next section of this weblog post will be dedicated to some of the other lessons and notable matters from this past month and for the impending end of the semester. Before I end, however, I must stress that the latter observations serve as a testament of God’s direct intervention in my life. It is through revealing my errors in multitasking that He has allowed me to process and write about the following matters with the clarity that they deserve. This revelation attests to how unimaginably blessed I am to concern myself with such comparatively light and personal concerns all at the same time.

### 30 April
The palette of greens, yellows, and reds that the budding trees now make on the landscape is becoming more monochrome. The leaves are flushing out with ever greater vigour. I can no longer recall the last day when temperatures have fallen to what I would consider cold compared to the early spring season. It is now the last day of April. I now can appreciate this temporal beauty much like I was perhaps one month ago thanks to the peace of mind that God has gifted me. Once again, He has also reinvigorated the joy and clarity in my writing that I hope will be reflected in the small remarks that follow.

A combination of reading mathematical papers for my applied mathematics class, problem-solving in multivariable calculus, and being exposed to new mathematical objects and ideas through both courses has only strengthened my affinity for what is—at its core—the study of order in the universe. The rigor of the mathematical proof demands a fullness and clarity of understanding that elicits an attitude towards learning to cherish. This sentiment has been reflected more concretely in a new best practice of mine that I discovered only in the last few problems sets for Math 21a. I have begun organising and explaining my work in a way that reflects the wording of a mathematical paper. *Clarity* of thought in the midst of the problem—and especially when reviewing it—has resulted.

This month has also marked the beginning of the “weather-permissive cold shower”. When outdoor temperatures are at or above what I consider to be a room temperature of 16 degrees Celsius (and perhaps even lower, depending on the indoor temperature), I have simply resolved to use cold water. This choice has marked a rediscovery of how wholesome showering with cold water can be for the mind and the body. Beyond a refreshment and *clarity* that this has brought me, a certain satisfaction in reflecting aspects of human life that are now largely forgotten has accompanied this. A fire would no longer be needed to heat water for bathing when outdoor temperatures get this warm. The weather-permissive cold shower almost emulates bathing in a natural body of water at this time of the year while undeniably saving on energy.

As I approach the end of my first year in college, some form of holistic reflection only seems appropriate. Yet I believe to have already captured much of what I have learned that is relevant to an external observer through the practice of writing weblog entries. A common theme in many of these reflections should be stressed here, however. That is the importance of choosing to live a relatively “quiet” life. By using this first year of college as an opportunity to focus fully on academics, with running and church activities being my only two “extracurriculars,” I have reached a point of eagerness to take on light term-time work next academic year as a means to contribute to society and to gain financial literacy. From my availability observation spreadsheet to a newfound resolution to use a reference-management platform, a true appreciation for the value of time has accompanied this all.

With one week of reading period ahead, an outline of a daily schedule and some general goals will be one of tomorrow’s tasks. More broadly, I have begun pondering goals for this summer such as reading a series of books that I have listed within the past months. I only note these matters here because I *should* have plenty of time to think about them. It is apparent, however, that the bane of multitasking within the past month has precluded these musings. I have only found the direction to ponder these within the past week.

My ability to formulate much of this weblog entry has been the result of less than 1 week of newfound focus. Throughout April, a perceived pressure to capture the passing spring through writing kept the necessity of starting this entry in the background of my mind. Yet, on Monday, I realised that this is something that happens naturally with focus. My writings from this past winter and early spring attest accordingly. On especially serene walk in Mount Auburn cemetery this Thursday afternoon, the sights and smells created by foliage after the day’s rain had passed was remarkable. The beauty of an overcast sky that had only just begun to thin only accentuated this. Only with the gift of time and the freedom from multitasking was I able to access—even if only for 20 minutes—a temporal serenity could be found nowhere else but in that patch of hallowed, relatively natural ground.

The peace of God extends far beyond these personal revelations. It reflects a grace beyond comprehension. The struggles that I have faced with a matter so relatively trivial as multitasking only evidence how my life is not my own. I will end by citing an excerpt of text that was shared with me on Sunday morning. [Proverbs 3:1-12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Proverbs%203:1-12&version=NRSVUE) pertains to my current writings to an extent that has only now been revealed to me. If my reader has gotten this far, a brief addition of the linked text to his reading is all that I request. The clarity referred to in that passage is precisely that which I hope to have captured in this weblog—this testimony of how such clarity has been made manifest.
