# On Browsing the World Wide Web and A Notice Regarding the 1.5-Month Suspension of English Weblog Entries

## 27 – 29 May 2026
### Revised 30 May 2026

Throughout my past two semesters of university studies, the overwhelming majority of the interaction I have had with the internet has been linked to coursework. It is true that much of this constraint was by choice. My activities of leisure, such as walking or writing weblog entries during weekends, precluded the time sinks of my former years, like the *idle* viewing of YouTube videos. I have previously written about my waning desire to spend the hours that I once did on Minecraft or Roblox approximately 1 year before graduating high school. I simply began to prefer engaging with literary works of merit as a more consequential and rewarding use of free time. I am not asserting that reading is mutually exclusive with more idle activities in one’s repertoire of hobbies, rather I must stress that my aversion to video games reflects a natural (and blissful) shift in personal preference that I cannot address in sufficient detail here.

Yet, within the past 5 months especially, I have found edification and leisure on many weekend evenings in an online activity akin to reading. So often have “.edu” web sites from a bygone era, along with scholarly databases that I have gained experience in navigating, characterised my more recent interactions with the internet. It seems that an intentional use of many of these resources, combined with a limited exposure to algorithm-based platforms, has garnered my appreciation of the internet as the incomprehensibly rich information superhighway that it is. This newfound understanding converges with a longstanding intrigue in the vestiges of the “old internet” that continue to be accessible on the World Wide Web.

The activity that I address in this piece might best be introduced through an idea captured by the term “web surfing.” Some have used this term to signify connecting to the internet and browsing the web for the interest in itself of discovering new web sites or information. In this sense, web surfing involves exploring the World Wide Web by relatively aimless navigation that only relies on hyperlinks found within web sites. The “surfing” that I elucidate here retains this notion of discovery while being inherently dependent upon a modern search engine. Additionally, my interactions with the World Wide Web in this manner have always centred around some goal or topic of interest. There must be something that is searched for, and that act of searching addresses the essence of this activity that I have delayed defining in detail.

I am referring to the treatment of web browsing as a form of reading about a matter of interest that simultaneously involves searching for web sites beyond the well-known and frequented ones that dominate human web traffic. This perusal of the search index pages beyond the first few links often yields modern—but merely obscured—sites on the web that are often informative and reputable. Many of hyperlinks found in the “Resources” page of this web site were first encountered through this process.

So often do these obscured web pages remain frozen in an era now long-passed, however, revealing the second component of this form of browsing that I have found recreation in. The Google desktop site possesses a “custom time frame” option in its search tools, and this allows one to limit his results to pages that were indexed even before the turn of the millennium. It is around time period—from roughly 1995 to 2006—that I often restrict the search results to when browsing a topic of interest for leisure. Returning to the idea of reading, the internet almost becomes an interactive book and time machine at once; countless, functional web sites exist that look the same as they did in the different world that was the early 2000s. These sites’ continued functionality not only allows one to learn from reading their contents, but finding such obscure resources is in itself a reward. Buried under potentially hundreds of pages on any modern search engine and referenced in few other places on the World Wide Web, the URLs of these sites hold enough value to be stored in a dedicated bookmarks folder or list of resources akin to that on this site.

A remarkable proportion of these web sites are hosted under a “.edu” or a “.gov” domain, offering a further boon of credibility. Upon reflection, a fitting explanation for this phenomenon may be found in the characteristic funding and stability of educational or governmental institutions to be capable of continuing to hosting web pages that are much older than I. In demonstrating the Google time frame search capability to a family member last week, for instance, I used the search term “cosmology” as a mere demonstrative example (observational cosmology intrigues me greatly, although I have not yet researched this domain in detail). Yet three of the hyperlinks on the first page of results were of enough interest to me that I saved them. I have yet to read these at the time of writing, although they are given below.

This leisurely activity is no replacement for reading a book. Yet, for those interacting with the internet in 2026, this form of browsing the internet as an act of recreation may be much rarer. Part of the reason why I have dedicated such a long weblog entry to the matter rests in the deeper implications of this observation. The focused “surfing” that I have so far described reflects a more practical and consequential mindset through which using the internet is approached. A YouTube video that I have watched long ago does justice to many of the ideas evoked by these musings with great brevity, which is an endeavour beyond the scope of this piece. I implore my reader to watch at least the first 5 minutes of the film [linked here]( https://youtu.be/QEJpZjg8GuA).

Both my more recent patterns of using the internet for coursework and this recreational web browsing share a common trait that I must finally address—the idea of going online (or, *on the line*) for a discrete and well-defined period of time. The home computer once ensured the prevalence of one, perhaps shared access point to the World Wide Web that would be conducive to a better internalisation of what one was doing when connecting to the internet. There may be a small tragedy lying in the fact that this notion of a well-defined status of being online has been lost for many individuals today. The appreciation of browsing the World Wide Web for recreation may have been lost with it.

I cannot diverge into the subject of constant connectivity through the smart phone. I cannot diverge into the absurdities that I have observed when one constrains his leisure activities that use the internet to the short-form video format. I can only add that, with the form of web surfing so far described, it is well-arguable that one’s time is not wasted. Neither can this browsing be done indefinitely. I have rarely, if ever, desired to browse in this manner for over 1 hour. Once enough has been read or another matter demands one’s attention, boredom will drive one to go offline. And it is this eventual boredom, it seems, that is both natural and good.

## 30 May

I intend to be proficient in French as a second language with near-native level fluency. Not only is the ability to study and communicate in a different tongue linked to a distant land a bliss in itself, but I have begun to see how a second language opens the door to a different world. Part of the reason why I have focused so much time within recent years to studying French rests in my hope to use this language to expand the impacts that I may have in my future profession—in whatever manner that may be. Another part rests in the ability itself to travel to and perhaps work in France or other Francophone countries in Europe if I chose to emigrate to the United Kingdom—an idea that I continue to entertain (albeit with lessening intensity).

This is all to preface that I will be absent from the United States, beginning this evening, until 11 July for a French language-intensive study abroad programme in Paris. That said, I plan to avoid exposure to my mother tongue as much as possible in order to take full advantage of this period of immersion. In addition to the 2 courses I will take, however, this immersion will not render endeavours such as practicing mathematics or reading in my free time any less wholesome or productive.

I will continue to write journal entries and occasional weblog posts in French between 31 May and 11 July. Sojourning in France will doubtless bring forth many reflections worthy of sharing on this web site. My reader may use an online translation tool to read these entries, if need be, although the nuances of vocabulary and the sense of some thoughts will probably be lost in said translation. English weblog entries will resume as early as my departure from Charles de Gaulle Airport on 11 July.

My aversion to air travel deserves its own entry, although I must conclude by reaffirming that the entire process of travelling over a long distance on an aeroplane is one most loathsome, unnatural, and taxing on the human body. It is not natural for one to be strapped in a sitting position for over 8 hours, travelling over several time zones with no temporal space to internalize the vast distances traversed. This is especially true when flying over the Atlantic Ocean from the United States to Europe. My recent voyage from Boston, Massachusetts to Erie, Pennsylvania on an Amtrak train served as an antithesis of the over-compression of distance, the inability to move, the environmental tolls, and the purgatory that is the airport for me. The adjustment of my digestive and sleep schedule to HAEC time for this upcoming flight began this morning.

Let this brief lapse into a polemic against the concept of long-distance air travel only accentuate my great anticipation for this study abroad opportunity. I can only continue to hold fearful thanksgiving and praise for God—regardless of the extreme privilege of attending this programme with full funding for the next one-and-one-half months. This Saturday morning is bright and serene, and I pray to return to this tranquil land with much to share for the benefit of others in July. May what the reader has gained from this piece and what will be learned during my studies this summer serve to further the glory of God.
