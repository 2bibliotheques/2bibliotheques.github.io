# On Night and a Semester Nearing Its End
## 15-22 November 2026

The week of the 16th to the 22nd of November has been fraught with above-average temperatures and no rain. While the landscape has no less of its November beauty, I can only await the coming of seasonal weather this coming week. After brief research, it appears that this widespread warmth marks only the beginning of the climatic impacts brought by the El-Nino Southern Oscillation, detailed in the NOAA forecast [hyperlinked here]( https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/fxus05.html). It is on the verge of Thanksgiving week that I publish this particularly long weblog entry, so much so that it necessitates a proper introduction. November has been no less dense with learning and fruitful hours, a truth that I hope is reflected in this piece. Here, I will first address the plethora of observations that have come to me following the daylight savings time change this year. The second half of this entry contains more general musings that I have resolved to share most recently on the gift that is every hour of daylight. Even so, my gravitation towards such thoughts is undoubtedly implicated in the darkness that begins to settle before 17:00 every day. I will end this piece by addressing events with far greater significance than that which they hold in my own life, ultimately leading to advice regarding the planning of one’s spring semester that may apply well beyond university students.

### 15—21 November
The morning of November 1st this year will remain memorable for me, as the transition to Eastern Standard Time genuinely caught me off guard. I likely saw the event on my calendar during the week and dismissed it as the change to Greenwich Mean Time in the United Kingdom, for this occurs one week before daylight saving in the United States. Nevertheless, I had been gifted an hour of time that morning as I realised that the reading of 05:45 on my laptop clock could not be erroneous. Yet the brightening dawn outside my dormitory window was just the same as it had been for the past month.

An equally memorable event transpired at the end of the same day. This experience provided the immediate impetus for this writing. “Meadowbrook Park” had caught my attention on Google Maps as a potential hiking destination several times in the past. In the late afternoon, I resolved to visit the seemingly poorly documented park with the hope that it contained a trail. After reaching the edge of Lower Mystic Lake and gazing for the first time over its waters, seeing a distant dam illuminated by the orange rays of the low-hanging sun, I was met with slight disappointment at the mere roadside embankment that appeared to constitute much of the park’s land area. (I now know that the park is better described as a morsel of conservation land that is [documented on the Town of Arlington web site]( https://www.arlingtonma.gov/town-governance/boards-and-committees/conservation-commission/conservation-land-stewards).)

However, this excursion is most memorable because of a resolution that struck me on my return trip. It is a resolution that my evening excursions on many Saturdays or Sundays past should have made clear. Well before I reached Lower Mystic Lake, thoughts of the limited time remaining in the evening would not allow me to be at full ease. Even if I were to find a significant trailhead that evening, the coming of the night would have invariably made the walk rushed. It thus became unmistakably clear; going for a hike or a walk under time constraints, especially the sunset, severely degrades the peace of mind that such an excursion into nature should bring. It is better to walk in an albeit-developed neighbourhood near home than to race the setting sun on a distant trail.

On that evening, however, I must have felt the very beginnings of a particular angst of mine as the ambient light began to dim. Enough light surely remained to return home safely, but I had still effectively made the same mistake that I made continually near the end of the fall semester last year. At some points last fall, I had been relatively far from home when darkness fully came. I felt the beginnings of the same dread of night on this evening, and it is this dread that will serve as the gateway to the remainder of this piece.

While my reader may find this supposed dread histrionic, I note that this dread first came upon me only last year, when I first found myself alone and a bicycle or subway ride away from my university home. I am aware that my personal preferences to labour and to be away from home in the sunlight that unequivocally contribute to this dread are not preferences shared by all. Yet daylight saving time has spurred several observations of mine concerning the coming of night that are valuable for the general reader to at least consider. I have already described the first of these observations. While some may find the need to return home with the sunset looming far less pressing than I do—especially if he is already in a familiar area with plenty of artificial lighting—the folly of travelling far for a walk under time constraints is universal.

As much artificial lighting strews the lands surrounding my university home, however, the night still brings forth a fundamental change in how one must operate. For the remainder of this semester, my weekday runs will largely be in the dark. As much as a headlamp and lighted vest make running the same distances and routes well-practicable—and while the ensemble of mental and physical boons brought by an exercise break remain steadfast—this past Friday evening revealed something that had come upon me incredibly subtly throughout my night runs of the past 2 weeks. I was able to return home and begin running at around 16:15, when the sunset was only imminent. As I began my run, the sight of the still-blue sky and of the route in relative brightness brought forth a conspicuous joy. I realised that I had unwittingly missed running in the light. Again, this is not to say that running at night is less enjoyable. But it is the certain subtlety of not knowing what one is lacking that my realisation invokes. I will continue to run in the evenings for the remainder of the semester given my class schedule, and joyously so. Yet the conspicuous appreciation of the gift of daylight hints to an idea much more profound.

Over one week has now passed since that Saturday morning run, when I finally considered a question that—upon reflection—surprises me to have never come across my mind before. At the time of writing, the morning sky begins to brighten near 05:45. When I traversed Brattle Street at 06:20, however, daylight proper had illuminated the empty expanse on both sides of me. As I ran through one of the equally quiet neighbourhoods on the route to the Charles River, I acknowledged for perhaps the first time what was the absurdity of the silence around me. In the late evening hours and into the night, these roads are *relatively* bustling with some form of activity. Perhaps it was because this was the morning following my aforementioned realisation that the question came upon me. Even if it was Saturday, I still found myself asking: why is it that human activity here runs well into the darkness of night, yet not a soul could now be found on the sidewalks when practically broad daylight illuminated the way? Why is it that waking at or before the break of dawn—when visibility is naturally restored—seems to be generally rarer than staying awake well after one must depend upon artificial lighting? These questions certainly implicate matters as various as the adoption of time zones and the psychological impact of artificial lighting. The extent to which people were yet to stir that morning struck me, nonetheless. Even if it had not been a Saturday morning, the relative quietness of the streets surrounding my dormitory in the dawn hours throughout the week begs the same question. 

I conclude these reflections on the coming of the night by observing that, evidently, the difference between darkness and daylight had much deeper implications to those living in the distant past. If I may share one further piece of advice on this matter, my “micro-adjustment” to make the most use of the daylight has proven mentally and physically beneficial. This adjustment has simply involved shifting my biological routines 15 minutes earlier, namely sleep and mealtimes. I have therefore only ever adjusted to 45 minutes of the daylight-saving time change rather than 1 hour. Although 15 minutes may seem rather trivial, (indeed, I do not get up any earlier in order to remain in-sync with my class schedule) being able to rise as the branches of the trees are mere silhouettes on the dark, blue-grey background of the morning twilight is a beauty that I would otherwise lose.

## 21—22 November 2026

“Everyday is a Gift” read a banner hanging on the exterior of a monument engraving business that I passed whilst cycling through Belmont last weekend. Regardless of the word-choice error, this reminder struck me as one that I needed. For I realised that I had often been neglecting to truly internalise this fact in the midst of my university routine. Perhaps one of the greatest ways to appreciate the current moment has become especially clear to me throughout the events of this past week. That being close observation of detail. Further context is due given how vague this idea may sound. This past week, a combination of having fully adjusted my coursework routines and the coming of Thanksgiving recess has left me with the most free time that I have had in months. And with this free time has come my immediate desire to *do more*. Only this past week did I perceive how much I have adjusted to the workload of this semester, particularly with the time taken by Physics 15a. For, the 3-4 total hours absorbed by the course laboratory component being freed for the remainder of the semester seems to have made much of this difference. (This lab has been a particularly tedious and ill-rewarding requirement; interestingly enough, it been consolidated into a separate class starting next year. I cannot diverge into my distaste for this course component here.)

As I have written about in [my previous weblog entry](https://2bibliotheques.github.io/2026/10/31/textbook.html), taking the time to observe small details can lead to profound insights. Musing upon what may easily be overlooked in the course of one’s day is also a way to appreciate the present moment in itself. The majority of deciduous trees in Cambridge have now lost all or most of their leaves, as is usual so late in November. A particular tree near on my dormitory grounds remains mostly green, however. I minded this fact only at the end of this week, despite having passed this tree daily. This small curiosity provides an example of how numerous are the small matters one can appreciate in only the course of a day.

With this in mind, two events this past week struck me with such profundity that I have chosen to record them here. In Physics 15a lecture on Thursday, part of the class centred around the universal constant of gravitation, G. Although the fact that this quantity is the same throughout the universe is in itself awe-inspiring, the demonstration for this lecture was most astounding. A full description of the experimental setup can be found at the [hyperlink here]( https://sciencedemonstrations.fas.harvard.edu/presentations/cavendish-experiment). In essence, our class observed oscillations magnified by a mirror and a laser that were caused by the gravitational attraction *between two metal masses*. And after over 1 hour of close observation, a member of the demonstration team used this to calculate the gravitational constant within a 2% margin of error. This experiment, made even more astounding by the fact that Henry Cavendish first used the same general setup to [mass the Earth in 1798]( https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2008/06/1798-cavendish-weighs-world), provides another analogue to what careful observation can yield when one takes a moment to observe small matters in his daily life. Our professor shared the article [hyperlinked here]( https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0431-5) to the class, showing how the same fundamental setup has been used in the most modern and precise measurements of G.

Our professor also shared [this Nature article](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-019-03332-7 
) that describes how a neural network was recently able to find that the Earth orbits elliptically around the sun using only observational data. The dreadful environmental, social, and educational impacts of large language models as they are so vigorously promoted by companies such as Meta is beyond the scope of this piece. It is in applications like those presented by this article, though, where machine learning proves to be an incredibly powerful and exciting tool. My resolve to become familiar with machine learning inasmuch as it may make unlock the otherwise infeasible formed on this day after I heard of the neural network’s results.

I stress that, in my current life, I find absolutely no need to use artificial intelligence. AI is no replacement for a textbook, for example. Yet this past Friday will forever go down in history as the day when Levent Alpöge announced his proof of the Riemann Hypothesis using his employer’s AI model. A Scientific American article discussing the verification of the proof may be found [here](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-ai-riemann-hypothesis-proof-is-almost-certainly-true-independent-mathematicians-attest/). Alpöge’s own remarks on the discovery may be found [here](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf), although his anthropomorphising of the model is rather disappointing. In the Math 101 proof workshop on Friday afternoon, our teaching fellow made the remark to the class that one need not doubt his efforts to learn mathematics if AI can seemingly do it all. This teaching fellow said that doing so would be akin to him questioning why he should have learned English if there will always be those more proficient in it than he is. Mathematics is a language, as the teaching fellow asserted. Indeed, one can only surmise where a language will take him. This might include using AI as the potent tool that it is. Yet it is a tool that must be used responsibly.

The sun has set this Sunday night, as I write after a visit to the Storer Conservation for the first time. This weblog entry might be the longest one yet; I both thank and apologise to any reader who has followed up to this point. The final part of this entry will accordingly be brief. I received an email earlier this month that conveys some of the realisations that I had when registering for next semester’s courses rather lucidly. The writer or writers of this issue of “Crimson Connection” for my university class of 2029 outline the prospects of our fourth university semester rather well. I have saved a PDF of this issue that is accessible [here]( https://2bibliotheques.github.io/assets/documents/Class%20of%202029%20Crimson%20Connection%20%E2%80%93%20November%202%20Issue.pdf). The next month yet lies ahead, however, and the week of reading period held at my university will provide time to study and learn. I will only add one more brief follow-up to my previous entry on the power of the textbook. I have only recently learned how to truly study using Morin’s *Problems and Solutions in Introductory Mechanics* for the Physics 15a midterm II that occurred this past Tuesday. I note this here because it is remarkable how effective it was to simply sit down and solving problems after re-reading the textbook introduction sections. And again, in the coming weeks, there will be more than enough time to do solve many more.

A final remark is in order, mostly for potential historical interest. The Harvard Graduate Student Workers Union strike continues. I have not had the hour-long section on Wednesdays for my general education class for over one month now. Although strike continuity has been maintained remarkably well for my 3 other courses, this strike has impacted other students much more significantly. I can only pray that an agreement will be made well before next semester. For more information regarding the strike, I refer my reader to the [university provost’s web site](https://academicunionization.harvard.edu/harvard-graduate-students-union-hgsu-uaw).
