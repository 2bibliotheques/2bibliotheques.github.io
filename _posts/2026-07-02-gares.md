# *La Province*, Studying in June, and *Les Gares de Paris*

## 27 June – 2 July 2026

### 27 July

On one morning early this week, the professor of our art history class began describing a cultural division that persists between Paris and the Province. According to this professor, Parisians view life in Province—that is, the majority of the country that lies outside of the [*Unité urbaine de Paris*](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit%C3%A9_urbaine_de_Paris)—as “boring.” His articulation of the contrary, Provincial perspective as a failure to understand why one would choose to live in such a crowded and stressful environment particularly resonated with me. I cannot attest to the veracity of this cultural division. Neither would I presume any individual’s mentality based upon such a generalisation, even if it seemed unmistakably true. Yet this discussion served as a striking reminder of the extent to which life in Paris contrasts with that in the expansive countryside—or even in a smaller city or town. My personal aversion to modern urban areas stems from a confluence of objective health concerns and strong personal criteria of what constitutes a happy life; further treatment of this matter is beyond the scope of this piece.

But I must pose a careful distinction between seeking an education in an urban
centre and subsequently choosing where to live. I will not try to maintain that a career search for those possessing—say—a university bachelor’s degree in mathematics would be nearly as easy in some of the quieter regions of Province as in Paris. Yet it is times like today that remind me why one’s quality of life must be just as, if not more important, than career opportunities when choosing where to live. I now write on the RER line D train returning from Coye-la-Forêt. Today marks the first and the only time that I will have left Paris during this sojourn. And the quietness whilst walking in Coye-la-Forêt or the surrounding trails was palpable. I have written before on the unfelt absence of the natural environment when occupied by an endeavour like school for weeks on end. Only when revisiting the natural environment in this state may one realise how he has been lacking its serenity. This is precisely what came upon me today once more in the verdure beside *La Thève*. However, for the first time in my life, the freshness of the air struck me as something to savour.

That said, for much of what is probably a large segment of the Parisian population employed in sectors such as technology or finance, living in the capital must be a choice. Perhaps it is this idea that has spurred such an extensive first part of this entry. An individual who values life in a quiet environment as I do may live well in a metropolis such as Paris with a fulfilling career. Yet it may be easy to forget how profoundly the other aspects of one’s life may improve upon returning to the tranquillity of a smaller settlement.

Education and work opportunities offer the strongest argument for choosing to live in a large city, yet resources such as [this web site oriented towards Parisians](https://paris-jetequitte.com/) attest to the fact that viable career opportunities do exist elsewhere (the title of this particular source is an allusion to [*Paris je t’aime*](https://parisjetaime.com/) from *l’Office de tourisme*, a resource that I have found useful for select self-guided walking tours).

### 30 June

Whilst traversing the Parisian streets in the morning and afternoon throughout the last four weeks, I often observed students walking to or from school. This has served as a periodic reminder that an education system foreign to me has defined the formative years of all those I see. Only after having settled fully into a coursework routine, however, did I internalise that I too have been engaged in a structured programme over the course of what may mark a period of relative academic stagnation for many in the United States. It came upon me that continuing to study in June in the capacity that this language-intensive programme has demanded is wholesome. I found a certain joy in the fact that my two summer courses do not end until well after even the majority of French students are on recess. The nationwide summer vacation period begins this Friday.

Behind this personal fulfilment, there doubtless rests the more general wholesomeness of a consistent labour and leisure routine. Yet another line of reflection that I have touched upon above has been evoked by these observations. That is, taking the time to study the education systems of France and of England may provide a remarkable means to better understand life in these countries. Once again, these education systems largely define the first twenty years of a citizen’s life.

In accord with the principle that an education and a fulfilling career provide the basis of happiness (to labour fruitfully is one of the greatest pleasures in life, as I have written many times before), this research endeavour has also revealed the need to reflect more deeply upon what one can learn from these education systems to inform efforts for systemic change. It is easy to find the faults in the education system of the United States. Yet it is this positive form of edification—that is, gaining a thorough familiarity with the education and subsequent career pathways for those like the French—that may at least be applicable to one’s personal life. For the remainder of my time as a university student, the summer will be used as an opportunity to labour. For students like those in England, this concept is arguably integrated into the school year.

### 1 July

There remain only two smaller notes regarding the past two weeks that are worthy of remark here. The first concerns a self-created tour of the 7 major train stations in Paris. The French language offers two commonly used words that may be used to refer to a train station, those being *la station* and *la gare*. One hears the former used when speaking of the copious amount of subway and bus stations throughout the city. These are the places where one purchases *un ticket* to travel relatively short distances. But the much larger train stations served by regional or international trains are instead be referred to as *gares*. Here, one purchases *un billet* to reach lands potentially thousands of kilometres from the urban centre. This distinction touches upon what I discovered is the continued importance of the 7 contemporary *gares de Paris* that are served by the *grandes lignes*. These monolithic structures attest to the continued importance of train travel in Europe whilst continuing to serve their historical purpose as the *portails* into the city. Their architectural beauty conveys a certain respect towards and an excitement for the modern innovation that is the passenger train.

While planning this tour, I assembled a document containing the 7 major train stations of Paris, a map of France depicting the regions serviced by trains originating from each station, and a brief description of each station’s current lines (all of which come from [this Wikipedia article](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liste_des_gares_de_Paris#Grandes_lignes)). Guided by this paper, I toured each station on my own with an interest in the buildings themselves as well as the trains that remain easily accessible in the *halls*. For nearly every station, in fact, one is able to walk directly up to the trains idling next to the *quais*. And this self-organised excursion over the course of last Friday and Sunday was even more rewarding than expected.

This virtually free tour not only revealed some of the most beautiful edifices in the city, but my resulting familiarity with the 7 great stations has solidified a sort of self-orientation within the city as well as an internalisation of the capital’s significance. For one who has the free time and wishes to explore Paris—or any other major city with such stations—this activity will likely prove well worth the time. My adoration of the train deserves an essay of its own, and it is certainly at play here. Yet, be it this idiosyncratic programme or one of the many other printable tours that I have done whilst in Paris ([this hyperlink](https://www.unjourdeplusaparis.com/paris-balades/promenade-dans-le-paris-medieval) contains a memorable example), the self-guided walking tour is a free and incredibly efficient manner to familiarise oneself with a large city on a weekend afternoon. 

The historic heatwave that has come upon France over the past two weeks additionally deserves a record. With temperatures well above 30 degrees over the night and reaching 40 degrees or more during the day, the *vague de chaleur* soon became a *canicule* of immense proportions. Despite the closure of schools and businesses, I maintain that the lack of a widespread adoption of air-conditioning in Paris remains a model of energy efficiency and environmental awareness that cannot receive enough justice in this piece. Beyond the drastic difference that fans make when used correctly, I have directly observed the importance of drinking large quantities of water. On the hottest days when the body needed need to transpire constantly without external cooling, I found myself drinking more than 1 gallon of water. This was accompanied by little to no change in the frequency of water closet visits. 

The repose offered by the relatively cooler weather throughout this week may yet be temporal. I have only today been made aware of another *vague de chaleur* that is expected to arrive next week. The public parks of Paris remain open for 24 hours after the first heat wave, and bathing in the Seine is expected to be permitted on July 4th. Yet, even in the face of another heat wave, my daily growth in the French language continues with striking comfort and a *natural* form of ease. I will not leave Paris with the ability to speak French fluently. Yet beyond the immense progress that complete immersion in French has brought over this past month, one of the greatest experiences of studying this second tongue in the capital has been learning how to learn the language henceforth. For this and for all of the observations herein contained, I once again give all glory unto God.
