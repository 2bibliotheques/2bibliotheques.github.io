# Caution: A Small Price to Pay — Reflections Upon a Bicycling Incident
## 18 March 2026

I am not writing this entry out of pleasure, nor do I intend to spend any more time doing so than what is strictly needed. However, the gravity of what occurred to me yesterday on my long bike ride to Lexington, Massachusetts demands careful reflection. I was nearly struck by a vehicle at an intersection on Massachusetts Avenue. Furthermore, it is objectively and completely my fault. This entry serves to cement a resolution that has fully emerged within me this morning while providing an indelible reminder of what my lack of cautiousness may have otherwise caused. 

Ever since I first forayed onto the bicycle lanes of Greater Boston, I have often observed cyclists crossing intersections at red lights when all vehicles are stopped. Any notion of justifying a similar habit should be expunged here; these individuals may very well fit the profiles of those lost to cyclist-induced collision deaths on the road. Yet it is this poor example that I had followed once more yesterday afternoon—only the intersection was open to vehicles on both sides.

With the drive to reach at my destination within the timeframe predicted by Google Maps ever present, I must have thought that I need not slow down. There were no cars visible on the side street, and I must have carelessly, idly, and stupidly reasoned that I could stop at moment’s notice if one appeared. A single grey SUV came into my peripheral vision when aborting the crossing was beyond reason. The driver of the SUV veered slightly whilst stopping suddenly enough that one could hear the dull sound of scraping rubber on asphalt. I shamefully looked back to see a horrified man with grey hair behind the windshield (only retirees should be away from their labours at this hour on a weekday, I later thought). This must have all occurred in less than 3 seconds.

In less than 3 seconds, my serene visit to Lexington could have been beyond thought. It is remarkable how quickly one’s circumstances may change due to an incident like this; yet the ease with which this close call could have been avoided points towards a life lesson that will forevermore be held as principle by me. I will now live by caution whenever the opportunity is presented to take it.

This is most immediately applicable to my excursions from home. I will now remain stopped at every single red light that I come across, even if I observe other bicycle riders actively crossing the intersection. Two incredibly specific exceptions exist to this rule: 

1. If the walk signs for the crosswalks that traverse the other road indicate that it is safe to pass, I may continue forward after stopping and looking carefully both ways. A green light for the road being travelled on typically follows.

2. There exist specific intersections that * *I am already familiar with* * where the red lights illuminate solely to allow pedestrians to cross. Otherwise, the bike lane follows the curb on one side where no road is present as to not cross any paths of vehicles * *with a wide margin* *. After stopping and looking carefully both ways at these specific and recognisable intersections, I may continue forward when the light remains red.

Cars and bicycle traffic should not be travelling on the same road. Part of my resolution includes a conscious recognition of the danger that is posed by every journey that involves facing the automobile, despite the existence of dedicated bicycle lanes. Beyond cycling, however, this event made me realise that I have been failing to heed to a lesson that I learned with difficulty many months ago. One should never idly assume that train lines will be open to him at all times. Before any excursion using the MBTA subway, it must remain a rigorous habit of mine to check the MBTA alerts page (which is saved and directly accessible on my cell phone home screen) before setting forth on any excursion involving public transportation. 

I have written in my first-year journal about another lesson that I must also follow with stronger rigor. That is to arrive early to any scheduled commitment so that the need to rush is precluded. I am most notably referring to the departure from one location to attend a class or an event at another when the task that I am pulled away from can easily be resumed in an empty lecture hall or wating area. Although this note may seem rather specific, simply packing and departing early enough to provide plenty of travel time oftentimes exemplifies the ease with which caution may be taken.

When one is gifted with the recognition of the need for caution, the investment of time or effort of taking heed is often nought compared to what is being risked. In cases like mine yesterday afternoon, it is plain stupidity to do otherwise. I have paid the price for this stupidity in the form of the time it took to write what has become a long piece. But that is nothing compared to what my mindlessness on the road could have cost. The gratitude that I give to God for avoiding potential death yesterday is only accentuated by my newfound sense of cautiousness.
