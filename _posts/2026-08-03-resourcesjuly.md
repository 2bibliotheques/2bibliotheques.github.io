# Resources for Language Learning and Personal Documentation

## 1 – 3 August 2026
### Revised 5 August 2026

It is often easy to read good advice without truly internalising its meaning or its significance. I have found this to be especially noticeable when reading advice that I could second to others only after having experienced its veracity firsthand. The lessons that one learns after overcoming great travail are often ingrained the deepest; this phenomenon holds especially true in educational endeavours where efficient use of one’s time is often a function of idiosyncratic learning preferences and needs. Acquiring a new language is a prime example. Yet after continuing to study French following one-and-one-half months of complete language immersion in Paris, I have found certain documented advice to be compelling enough to share here. It is hoped that those who are attempting to master another language may find something valuable from this entry that does not require studying abroad to understand and appreciate.

In the first part of this piece, I will share two sets of language learning advice—one from an online resource that I have recently found and one that I have produced whilst studying in Paris. Although both resources are in French, the advice is so valuable and so applicable to all language learners that running the content of each through a translator will prove well worth one’s time. The cognitive boons and the wholesome joy of acquiring another tongue are beyond the scope of this piece. Yet it is hoped that my readers who are not studying a foreign language will find that many of the pedagogical or practical points contained in the two resources apply to almost any life endeavour. Nevertheless, I will set forth the pragmatism of keeping certain personal documentation in the second part of this entry. This applies to practically any inhabitant of the modern world.

## Two French Resources for Learning Any Language

With that said, the remarkable degree of comfort and *naturalness* in engaging with the French language that I have noted in my previous weblog entry has continued despite me being broken from immersion for several weeks now. My studies of a few unknown or shallowly understood grammatical topics seem to be much clearer, and they are oftentimes reinforced by daily consumption of French video content that follows in the evening. As my tutor for the study abroad program noted during one of our final sessions, learning another language is not like studying mathematics. Only after several weeks of immersion in French could I understand what precisely she meant. This is why, to one who may be studying a foreign tongue on his own, I implore—continue. In those moments when one’s language studies seem inefficient or like a waste of time, there is an assurance that I can now attest to. Previously studied grammatical minutiae will resurface remarkably easily when one is further along the learning curve, and it these nuances then be mastered thanks to such previous exposure. 

Complete immersion in a language must be the most effective and profound way to develop language studies into the pursuit of mastery. Although one-and-one-half months was enough in my case to cause the paradigm shift to comfort in the language that every learner may eventually experience, even a few days of complete immersion exponentiates one’s progress in a language as long as this immersion is embraced. I can now advise every foreign language learner to consider the importance of this immersion along with the value of every preceding hour of study that enables one to take complete advantage of his time abroad. Of course, I am well aware that not every one of my readers will have the opportunity to experience complete foreign langue immersion in the near future.

These individuals may find the following resource especially useful. I can most immediately assure that this resource is useful for those who possess a similar level of French fluency to me and who have had an experience of complete immersion. But following this advice when studying in an anglophone country could save hours of time for other learners. An optimal language studying routine is highly individualised, and along with the plethora of other lived experiences in learning French that I could share here, it would be better if my reader referred to a five-day language “course” that comes in the form of video lessons and short exercises. 

Although I cannot attest to the paid content offered by *Français avec Pierre*, their free YouTube videos originally intrigued me as a high-quality means to review or learn French in a manner that mimics how I came to listen to my language professor during the summer programme. I can neither attest to the quality of the advice given in the beginner-level video series. The intermediate and advanced-level videos, however, cover how to develop one’s competencies in each area of production and comprehension. I can second almost every point that Pierre addresses based upon my own experience in Paris. The hyperlinks to receive one lesson per day via email may be found below. Once again, even those readers studying another language will find invaluable advice with the help of subtitles and text translation.

[Français avec Pierre : Cours de français gratuit au niveau intermédiaire / avancé]( https://www.francaisavecpierre.com/inscription-cours-fr/)

[Français avec Pierre : Cours de français gratuit au niveau débutant]( https://www.francaisavecpierre.com/cours-de-francais-debutants)

## On Personal Documentation

The unparalleled value of writing as a form of developing and fixing one’s reflections should become axiomatic when reading this weblog. Indeed, my writings thus far have fulfilled and even exceeded the roles ascribed to them in the [Introduction to This Weblog]( https://2bibliotheques.github.io/2026/02/22/introduction.html). Writing in this manner is enjoyable and wholesome for the mind, and it produces materials that wax ever more valuable as time passes. Taking the initiative to produce careful personal documentation in other, perhaps more mundane forms will similarly accrue invaluable information. Through sharing several examples of my own personal records that I now appreciate greatly, I will clarify the sort of documentation that I am referring to. These examples also show how the tedium or apparent unproductivity that one may feel when first establishing some of these records will soon be overshadowed.

One of the most tedious of my personal documents to establish is now one of the easiest to maintain. In anticipation of accumulating a vast collection of books and physical media over my lifetime, I developed a catalogue of my “personal library” when time permitted over last winter recess. Every book, DVD, CD, and Blu-Ray disc that I own is now recorded in a Microsoft Excel sheet. This record includes a simple catalogue numbering system from which one may determine the format of every material. The ability to determine the exact edition of a piece of media that I own along with all pertinent information relating to the object is now a matter of executing a CTRL+F command on a table. Here is the time-saving elegance of the computer at its finest, only inferior to its paper counterpart by the need to keep and maintain backup copies. Even then, as I have learned when digitising other records, the Excel table may always be printed. A template for this catalogue is provided in a .XLSX file hyperlinked below. As a means to reinforce vocabulary, note that I chose to produce my catalogue in the French language.

[Le modèle du catalogue de la bibliothèque personnelle de Adam Hamilton (fichier .XLSX)]( https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1wOxYhvrsAJKrpMSv2HTzpInvCtnk84Zn/edit?usp=drive_link&ouid=114436756857963989891&rtpof=true&sd=true)

In the spirit of consistent, scientific observation, I recorded the time it takes to travel to various locations in Cambridge from my dormitory throughout last semester. A well-documented median figure for walking, bicycle, and train travel times proves useful in facing the reality that is the commute. (Although, the duration of most weekday trips with which I would be concerned in Cambridge pales in comparison to the time that one loses in extended automobile commutes. This is a topic for another piece.) With only a stopwatch and a spreadsheet program, one may accumulate a record of the travel times, travel dates, and other circumstances (such as bike unlocking times) of his voyages; these are later useful for irrefutable citation. A hyperlink to a simple Microsoft Excel template adapted directly from my record is provided below.

[Travel Times Template (.XLSX)]( https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1madNti9MN8gb7mKPnMmBVbY4jzubhi1_)

Another watch of mine has made it possible to accumulate a much more information-dense record. Weekly syncing of my Garmin Forerunner 205—a running watch from 2009 that I received like new as a Christmas gift from my grandparents in 2024—has resulted in a long log of running routes on my Garmin Connect online account. I spent an hour this week perusing and organising this list. I cleared my watch memory on the same day, keeping entries from only the past month out of those dating back to December of 2024. I did not clear the memory before I exported all of these runs as a .CSV file. A backup now exists should the Garmin Connect website become inaccessible.

It is continually astounding to view information ranging from my pace on portions of the exact routes that I ran to the weather conditions during the entire run. Routes from my recent sojourn in Paris and those from my holiday in the United Kingdom with my sister and brother-in-law remain there. Although I have no template to share, I can advise one who has a similar running watch to make a similar effort to back up his runs.

Finally, and most importantly for the exigence of this piece, I must stress the value of recording all of one’s online accounts after having recently done so myself. This is both the most valuable and potentially the most tedious personal record that I will share here. If my reader takes no action in response to this piece up to this point, let him take heed to this advice to create a similar account list. A record of online services with which one has registered an account for, along with the associated emails and passwords for each, is an immediately useful tool for accessing each account. The act of reviewing each of one’s online accounts is in itself a practice of personal data awareness. It provides a systematic means to delete the accounts that one no longer needs or to update the information on existing ones. This backup is also an invaluable asset to possess in the modern data privacy landscape. The established list embodies a sort of personal information footprint that could be useful in ways yet unforeseen.

As much as one may dismiss cataloguing every online account of his as infeasible or too time-consuming, the list does not need to be created in one sitting. So often are endeavours with great final products a matter of making incremental (but consistent) contributions over time. Further still, there are likely one or two online accounts that I have missed in my own record. But once the list is built, it need only be maintained.

My own list is organised across two Microsoft Excel sheets in one .XLSX file. One sheet contains a list of “lower level” accounts that may be accessed with passwords that I have memorised. These passwords are simply represented by shorthand notes. The other sheet contains essential information for “higher level” accounts with complex passwords that are indexed on paper. I now provide one final template below, adapted directly from my list. It contains an example entry for both sheets.

## Updates to the Resources Page on This Site

Since the inception of this web site, the “Resources” page has been updated with many hyperlinks that are highly useful or informative. Perhaps this page serves as yet another type of personal documentation worth constructing; it reflects my various web browser bookmark folders. The web page actually provides more information thanks to short descriptions of select resources.

Along with all of the resources that I have shared in this entry, I have added a short documentary from the French channel ARTE that raises several extraordinary points regarding the proliferation of generative artificial intelligence in recent years. It is yet another resource that is worth my reader’s time to consult using subtitles. One of the most striking points—one that has surfaced in other French sources regarding AI that I have consulted—addresses the incredibly narrow vision of artificial intelligence in the United States that is directly linked to the unnecessary consumption of computing power and the resulting rise of new data centres.

Companies such as OpenAI or Meta are vying for consumers to use their omnipotent chatbot models that consume gigawatts of energy. Anne Alombert, the author and philosopher who was interviewed in the video, propounds that AI has immense potential through specialised models that are significantly computationally lighter. She notes that “artificial intelligence” is itself a term that anthropomorphises and depoliticises the technology. A hyperlink to this video may be found [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ASciuwRveAg).
